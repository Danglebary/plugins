# Interrogate a ticket before implementing it, and give a killed ticket somewhere to land

From a deep review of the `work-plugins` fork, whose spec 004 built a `/spike` that does something pirr has no vehicle for: it takes an **existing ticket** and interrogates its claims before any code is written. The fork's framing is that a ticket is often a validated *symptom* fused with an unvalidated *problem* and a half-thought *solution*, sometimes polished enough to defeat surface judgment — its calibration pair is one ticket that read as impeccably groomed (file references, enumerated problems, a proposal list) yet prescribed per-symptom patches for an unnamed structural cause and ballooned into three PRs, against one that named the root cause with its mechanism and quantified blast radius. Nothing in that workflow recorded the investigation that would have caught the difference.

Note the naming collision up front: pirr already has a "spike" in `/to-spec`'s fit check, but it is a *pre-scope* vehicle — an open question, findings written to `docs/spikes/<slug>.md`, no ticket, no ceremony. The fork's is a *post-scope* vehicle: a ticket already exists and is being put on trial. They are complementary, and this needs its own name (`/vet`, `/interrogate`, `/probe`) rather than an overload.

For a personal-projects workflow the value inverts in an interesting way and gets **stronger**, not weaker. At work you distrust a ticket because someone else wrote it. Here *you* wrote it — usually during a `/to-tickets` decomposition pass, at spec altitude, before touching the code the ticket concerns — which means you already believe its problem statement and have no adversarial pressure on it at all. The discipline is three ordered questions, and the ordering is the whole point because only a validated symptom makes the next question answerable: is the symptom real and current, observable and traceable through the code path that would produce it; does the named cause actually produce it, and do the symptoms share a cause the ticket never names; does the sketched solution address the validated problem or patch around the symptom. Findings are appended to the artifact as they resolve, stage-tagged, so a resumed session finds its place by scan rather than by re-reading prose.

The verdict vocabulary is four shapes and reads as genuinely complete: **confirmed** (the scope stands 1:1 — proceed to implementation), **decomposed** (the validated problem splits into several tickets), **not-worth-doing**, and **epic-shaped** (this is spec-scale, route to `/to-spec` in the same session). That last arm is the one I'd expect to fire most often on personal projects — "this is bigger than I thought" is the most common honest outcome of ten minutes of investigation, and today it has nowhere to go but an abandoned branch. Two constraints do heavy lifting: the deliverable is **findings, never code** — anything executable built to validate a claim stays untracked scratch, and the branch is never reused for the implementation — and the lifecycle marker flips **last**, after the trace lands on the original ticket, so a crash can never leave a spike concluded while the ticket it judged sits silently abandoned.

**This collides with a real gap that is worth fixing whether or not the spike ships.** `TICKET-FORMAT.md` pins the status vocabulary to `open | in-progress | done` and explicitly forbids adding values; abandonment is structural and exists only at *spec* scope (the `_abandoned/` move). So a ticket that investigation kills, or that a decomposition supersedes, has exactly two landing places: deletion, which destroys the record, or `done`, which lies. The fork solved this by widening its status vocabulary to include `won't-do` and `superseded` as first-class **terminal** states, queryable without reading the body — a small change that is what makes the decomposed and not-worth-doing verdicts representable at all. That slice is cheap, independently valuable, and probably the first ticket of any spec this becomes.

One design call I'd flag rather than inherit: the fork mandates a two-session hand-off (stand up a distinguished branch and worktree, exit, re-enter inside it), which buys context hygiene at real ceremony cost against employer-specific worktree tooling. Keep the distinguished branch shape and the never-implement-here rule — those are what let downstream skills refuse to adopt a spike branch, and they're nearly free — but the mandatory session exit should probably be an offer here, not a requirement. Related: the fork banked `spike-branch-write-path-guard` because its branch-keyed skills (`/handoff`, `/close-out`, `/refactor`) all treated "exactly one artifact matching the current branch" as license to proceed and would happily commit from a spike branch; this repo's `/done`, `/refactor`, and `/next-ticket` resolve the same way, so the guard is scope here, not a follow-on.

Open questions at promotion: the name; whether the terminal-status slice ships standalone first; whether findings live in the ticket file under a recognizable lead-in (which then needs an exclusion rule so `/next-ticket`'s research opener and `/done`'s conformance judge don't swallow findings as acceptance criteria) or in `docs/spikes/` alongside the existing pre-scope artifact; and where the entry checkpoint sits — the fork put a shape-read at `/pickup`'s ticket read that *recommends and pauses*, never refuses and never auto-invokes, which maps cleanly onto `/next-ticket`'s existing research sub-agent step.
